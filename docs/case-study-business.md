## How the business actually runs

A fleet of fifteen agents can build things fast. But fast is worthless if the
work is not what the customer asked for, priced fairly, and delivered honestly.
So the business side of Asbury Solutions runs on a few plain rules.

### Every engagement starts as a written proposal

No verbal handshake deals. When a customer raises a problem, the first
deliverable is a short written proposal:

- what we will do, in plain language;
- what it costs;
- what "done" looks like — the acceptance test the customer can check
  themselves;
- what happens next.

A proposal is a promise on paper. That discipline forces the hard questions
up front — scope, price, deadline — instead of discovering them mid-project.

### Nothing ships before it is verified

The same fail-loud rule that runs the machines runs the business. A feature is
not "done" because an agent says so; it is done when the verification passes
and a human owner has signed off. For customer-facing work, the sequence is
always the same:

1. draft,
2. present to the owner,
3. owner confirms,
4. ship.

There is no step five. Nothing goes to a customer without that confirmation.

### Money is recorded only when it actually arrives

The books show real numbers or nothing. Money is not booked on a signed
proposal or a promising pipeline — it is booked when payment arrives and the
owner confirms it. An engagement that closes in the CRM but has not paid is
not money in hand; it is a promise. This keeps every metric honest, which
matters more than making any single number look good.

### Storefronts and products are tracked like inventory

Digital products sell through storefronts, and every listing behaves like a
SKU on a shelf: what it is, what it costs, whether it is live, how it sells.
No listing drifts, no price changes silently. If a product is sold, the
transaction and the fulfillment are both recorded.

### The confirm-gate is non-negotiable

The person who owns the company approves every customer-facing send, every
price, and every external commitment. Agents can draft, propose, and prepare —
they do not unilaterally commit the company. That is not bureaucracy; it is
what lets one person run a fifteen-agent company without losing control of
the front door.

## What this means for a customer

When a customer works with Asbury Solutions, the experience is ordinary in the
best way:

- they get a clear proposal they can actually read;
- the work is delivered when it is real, not when it is announced;
- the bill matches the quote;
- the product keeps working after handoff, because the same monitoring that
  runs the company watches the customer's system too.

The technology behind it — fifteen agents, one machine — is unusual. The
business conduct is not. That contrast is the point.
